Section A — Donor psychology, applied

This section is not a Donor Psychology 101 primer. It maps the established empirical research on philanthropic motivation directly onto the 7 candidates we're cultivating, with operational implications per candidate.

A.1 The empirical foundation

Prince & File's Seven Faces of Philanthropy (1994) remains the canonical typology, validated across thirty years of subsequent research:

  1. Communitarian — "Doing good makes sense" — gives to local causes that improve their community
  2. Devout — "Doing good is God's will" — religious/spiritual motivation
  3. Investor — "Doing good is good business" — tax-strategic, ROI-minded, metrics-driven
  4. Socialite — "Doing good is fun" — social belonging, galas, networks
  5. Repayer — "Doing good in return" — gives to institutions that helped them or family. Dominant in hospital mega-giving.
  6. Altruist — "Doing good feels right" — modest, quiet, mission-focused
  7. Dynast — "Doing good is a family tradition" — multigenerational

Bekkers & Wiepking's eight-mechanism meta-analysis (2011) identified the operational drivers: awareness of need, solicitation, costs/benefits, altruism, reputation, psychological benefits, values, efficacy.

The hospital-mega-gift evidence base (per Phase 4 benchmarking memo synthesis of 8 case studies): the Repayer + Dynast composite covers ~70% of $25M+ regional health system mega-gifts. Pure Investor (Icahn-type) is rare and requires sophisticated institutional architecture (research institutes, named schools) to convert. Pure Altruist or Socialite cases are essentially absent at the $25M+ tier.

Sargeant & Shang on solicitation effectiveness: peer-asked outperforms development-officer-asked by ~3x on closure probability. Anchoring research confirms donors give within 10-30% of the specific ask number; vague asks underperform by ~40%.

A.3 Operational implications by face

For each motivation type, the moves that work and the moves that break trust:

Repayer-dominant (Bauer)

  • What works: Anchor cultivation on the originating loss/recovery experience. Build the conversation around what the institution did or could have done. Frame the gift as a chosen continuation of an existing personal investment.
  • Anti-patterns: Generic mission language; treating the originating story as just biography rather than the actual motivation engine; transactional naming pitches without the personal anchor.

Devout (Bauer secondary)

  • What works: Theological-stewardship-of-vocation framing. "Sunday-Monday connection" language. Endowment / perpetuity structures. Recognize the spiritual dimension explicitly.
  • Anti-patterns: Pure secular impact metrics; minimizing the religious/spiritual dimension as if it were a footnote.

Dynast-dominant (Dyson, Whittingham)

  • What works: Multi-generational framing. Co-architect roles for next-gen family members. Family-named opportunities that endure for generations. Anniversary moments.
  • Anti-patterns: Single-generation framing; treating principals as decision-makers without engaging the next generation; pitching renaming or unbranding what the family already named.

Investor-dominant (Dalio, Rees)

  • What works: Rigorous impact metrics. Theory-of-change articulation. ROI framing. Co-architect roles in institutional strategy. Specific outcome-reporting commitments.
  • Anti-patterns: Soft mission language; aspirational framing without measurement infrastructure; treating their analytical orientation as cynicism.

Communitarian (Goldman Fowler emerging, Jasper Johns emerging, Bauer/Dyson secondary)

  • What works: "World-class care in our community" framing. Local-pride dimension. Specific geographic identity ("Hudson Valley families don't need to drive to Manhattan for cancer care"). Community-recognition platforms.
  • Anti-patterns: National-prestige framing; comparing the local institution unfavorably to flagship academic medical centers.

Altruist (Goldman Fowler, Jasper Johns)

  • What works: Low-recognition giving structures. Anonymous-option naming. Mission-focused framing. Quiet stewardship over visible recognition events.
  • Anti-patterns: Aggressive recognition push; gala-centric stewardship; pressuring for public visibility.

Section B — Cultivation cadence

B.1 Overview

Across the 8 benchmark cases studied (Phase 4), every transformational gift took ≥ 4 years from first qualified engagement to closed gift, and most took 8-12 years. The cadence below is calibrated to a $25M+ ask. For escalation gifts to already-active donors (Bauer), the cadence is shorter.

B.2 Phase 1 — Qualification (months 0-3)

Goal: Establish actual interest, confirm capacity, map motivations. The goal is not to ask; it's to learn enough that the candidate moves to active portfolio or gets dropped.

Touchpoints:

  • Warm-intro outreach via the connector identified in their dossier
  • Informal coffee or lunch
  • Hospital tour if natural
  • Stewardship visit if already-active donor

Success criteria for qualification:

  • Candidate is genuinely interested (not just polite)
  • Capacity confirmed at the relevant gift level
  • Primary motivation hypothesis tested and either confirmed or revised
  • Decision: advance to Phase 2 cultivation or drop

B.3 Phase 2 — Cultivation (months 3-12)

Goal: Build relationship and demonstrate institutional fit. Touchpoints that leave the candidate feeling smarter or more connected, not solicited.

Touchpoints by candidate type:

For already-engaged donors (Bauer, Dyson, Whittingham, Dalio):

  • Stewardship reports on prior gifts
  • Clinical-leadership introductions tied to their cause
  • Named-physician dinners
  • Family / next-generation engagement (Dyson, Bauer, Whittingham)
  • Anniversary or milestone moments (Whittingham 30th-anniversary 2027)

For new candidates (Rees, Goldman Fowler, Jasper Johns):

  • Strategic-advice conversations
  • Site visits to programs of specific interest
  • Peer-donor introductions (Rees: Feinstein board peers; Goldman Fowler: Northwell trustee peers via Lloyd)
  • Co-architect roles where applicable (Rees: research-thesis development; Goldman Fowler: women's health case design)

Critical principle (universal): Cultivation touches must be value-additive to the donor, not extractive. Each touch should leave them feeling they gained, not gave.

B.4 Phase 3 — Solicitation (months 12-18)

Goal: The formal ask.

Composition of the ask (per Phase 4 benchmarking: modal 4-person team):

  • Institutional CEO or system CEO (D'Angelo for system-level signaling; Robertson for Nuvance-level)
  • Lead clinical leader (CMO; named-chair holder; institute director)
  • Major-gifts officer (Laura)
  • Trustee peer or peer-donor (per candidate; see network map)

Location: Their setting (their office, their home), not yours.

The specific ask: Specific number, specific named opportunity, specific timeline, written proposal left behind. Anchoring research: donors give within 10-30% of specific ask amount; vague asks underperform by ~40%.

B.5 Phase 4 — Stewardship (months 18+)

Goal: Position for a second (usually larger) gift.

Touchpoints:

  • Named-recognition event designed around the candidate's preferences (for Altruists: low-key; for Dynasts: family-inclusive; for Communitarians: community-inclusive)
  • Impact reporting on a defined cadence
  • Continued access to clinical leadership
  • Family engagement where relevant
  • Anniversary moments (5-year, 10-year)

Critical principle: The second gift is usually larger than the first if stewardship is excellent, and zero if it isn't. The Icahn arc (2004 $25M → 2012 $150M → $250M+) is the canonical example.

C.2 The "no-ask" first meeting

Research consistently shows the highest-converting initial meetings are framed as advice or insight requests, not solicitations.

Sample framing template: "We're rethinking our [program/strategy] post-merger and Laura's leadership specifically wants to get your perspective given your work on [their specific area]."

Why this works: Activates the donor's expertise/status motive rather than triggering solicitation defenses. Particularly powerful for Investor archetypes (Dalio, Rees).

C.5 What to do when the answer is "no, not now, or radio silence"

For "no": Thank them sincerely. Continue gentle stewardship-style engagement (sharing relevant institutional news every 6 months). Do not re-ask for 18-24 months unless circumstances change materially.

For "not now": Ask what would need to be different. Set a re-engagement timeline they agree to. Honor it precisely.

For radio silence: Three follow-ups maximum over 60 days, then move to long-cycle stewardship (3-month check-ins via institutional news).

For candidates currently in this state in our cohort: none, all 7 are actively engageable as of this playbook.

Section D — Pitch architecture

D.1 Structural framework for the $25M+ solicitation conversation

Opening (first 15 minutes)

Center the donor's why, not yours. First 15 minutes are about their experience, motivation, and connection. Never lead with institutional needs.

Per-candidate opening framing — see cheat sheets for specific opening questions.

Bridge (next 15 minutes)

Connect their why to a specific opportunity. The named opportunity, tied to their motivation hypothesis — not Nuvance's biggest institutional need.

Per-candidate bridge framing:

  • Bauer: "What if the NICU Carol helped name became permanent — endowed in perpetuity, the staff secured beyond any campaign cycle?"
  • Dyson: "What if the Dyson Cancer Care brand expanded to a multi-facility presence under the integrated Northwell-Nuvance system — with Anne E. Dyson named in oncology research?"
  • Dalio: "What if the Hudson Valley / Western Connecticut underserved-population case for health equity became its own institutional model — different from the academic-medical-center model at NYP?"
  • Whittingham: "What if 2027's 30th anniversary marked the family's first multi-generational endowed chair — Andrew and Michael named together — with Adam and Paul as co-architects?"
  • Rees: "What if a Rees-anchored Nuvance-Feinstein research institute became your structural research platform — the way your Pitt $30M is becoming Pitt's recruiting platform?"
  • Goldman Fowler: "What if the unnamed Vassar Brothers Patient Pavilion's women's-health component became Goldman/Fowler Women's Health Center — a place that knits family heritage and reproductive-justice values into permanent infrastructure?"
  • Jasper Johns / Conley Rollins: "What if the Foundation's recent Sharon Housing Trust grant became part of a Sharon-community-infrastructure pattern — including the hospital community fought to keep?"

The specific ask (5 minutes)

Exact amount, exact named opportunity, exact timeline, exact pledge structure.

Anchoring principle: Donors give within 10-30% of specific ask amount. Vague asks underperform by ~40%. Ask high but defensible.

Anticipated objections (5-10 minutes)

Common objections to expect, with research-backed handling:

"Why Nuvance over [flagship]?" (most common for Dalio, Goldman Fowler, Rees) → Response framed per candidate: For Dalio, the underserved-population geographic differentiation. For Goldman Fowler, the cousin-Northwell-system-extension. For Rees, the Nuvance-region-as-extension-of-Feinstein-research-thesis.

"We already give to [other institution]." → Complement, not compete. "Our work is complementary — we [specific complementary value]. Many of our largest donors also give to [other institution]. Different gifts serve different missions."

"We need to talk to our advisors." → "Absolutely. I'd love to make that easier — I can send a proposal package your advisors will find well-structured."

The close

Silence after the ask. The development officer who speaks first after the ask loses ~30% of expected gift size on average.

Walk-away clarity

At the end, both parties leave with explicit clarity:

  • What's agreed
  • When you'll be back
  • What the candidate commits to in the meantime

Case Study — Lisa & Mark Schwartz $10M gift to Northern Westchester Hospital (2025)

Gift size: $10,000,000 (announced April 24, 2025) Type: Principal — the largest single gift in the hospital's 109-year history Compiled: 2026-05-11

1. The gift in one paragraph

In late November 2024, immediately after co-chairing the Northern Westchester Hospital (NWH) annual gala — which had just set the hospital's all-time fundraising record at $2.5M for a pediatric behavioral health initiative they had themselves anchored — Lisa Schwartz sat down with NWH President Derek Anderson and asked, "What's next?" Five months later, on April 24, 2025, Northwell publicly announced the Schwartzes' $10M commitment to fast-track NWH's Level III-N trauma designation, expand neurology and epilepsy services, and enhance neurovascular and stroke care — the largest single gift in the hospital's history and a direct, repeatable template for principal-gift escalation inside a Northwell-owned community hospital. [Sources 1, 2, 3, 5]

2. Donor profile

  • Lisa Schwartz: Founder/proprietor of Rainbeau Ridge Farm in Bedford Hills, NY; long-time advocate for global and local health; trustee of the NWH Board of Trustees (appointed 2018, announced jointly with Daren Wu, MD by then-Chair Michael Schwartz — no relation); member of the NWH Community Foundation Board (since 2018); co-chair of the NWH Community Health Committee; former chair and current board member of Seed Global Health; Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health Board of Dean's Advisors; trustee of Teach for China; trustee of Westchester Land Trust; founder of the PreSchool Connection; former Bedford 2020 director. [Sources 4, 9, 12]
  • Mark Schwartz: Former Vice Chairman of Goldman Sachs (2012-2016) and Chairman of Goldman Sachs Asia Pacific; previously oversaw Soros Fund Management's fund and philanthropy arms; co-founder of MissionPoint Capital Partners; founder of Taj Capital (2006); Harvard Business School alum; trustee of New York-Presbyterian Hospital (Executive Committee), Massachusetts General Hospital, MIT, and Harvard University. Joined the NWH Board of Trustees in the early 2000s; served three terms including as Vice-Chair. [Sources 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]
  • Family/wealth source: Long-term Bedford Hills, NY residents. Wealth is finance-derived (Goldman/Soros/MissionPoint). Wife-led day-to-day philanthropic engagement; husband validates and brings institutional credibility (NYP, MGH, Harvard, MIT trusteeships).
  • Prior philanthropic history at NWH (~25-year ramp):
    • 2000: Established the hospital's first endowment for nursing professional development — supported three Magnet recognitions [Sources 4, 9, 12]
    • February 2015: $1M for surgical services modernization (six new ORs opened 2016; ceiling-mounted technology; advanced spine/neuro/ortho/breast/robotics programs) [Sources 8, 11]
    • 2024 (gala-anchor): $1M anchor gift toward a $7M pediatric behavioral health initiative (child/adolescent psychiatric practice; stand-alone behavioral health crisis center; intensive outpatient program — phased rollout starting 2025) [Sources 9, 13]
    • April 2025: $10M lead principal gift (this case study) [Sources 1, 2, 3]
  • Primary motivation (Prince's framework): Communitarian (primary) + Dynast/Investor (secondary). Lisa's own words make the communitarian frame explicit: "For us, excellent health care is an essential pillar of a thriving community." [Source 1] She also articulates a distinct investor-philanthropist self-concept: "Mark and I tend to be drawn to projects and ideas that are emerging and that need funding, but no one is there yet." [Source 9] Mark's framing — "Philanthropy is an important part of living a meaningful life" [Source 2] — codes Repayer/Devout. Critical for Nuvance: this couple's repeated pattern is fund the unfunded, then escalate when it's working — not naming-rights vanity. The $10M was NOT structured around a named building.
  • Evidence for classification: 25-year giving arc at a single community hospital (rare among finance-wealth donors of Mark's institutional stature, who typically concentrate at NYP/MGH); residence in Bedford Hills, where NWH is the de facto community hospital; Lisa's farm-based community work; pattern of anchoring emerging programs (nursing endowment when nursing endowments were rare; behavioral health when post-COVID pediatric demand was emerging).

3. Recipient institution profile (at time of gift)

  • Northern Westchester Hospital, Mount Kisco, NY: Founded 1916; 245-bed, all-private-room not-for-profit community hospital serving Northern Westchester, Putnam County, southern Dutchess County, and parts of Fairfield County, CT (note: same Fairfield County CT service-area overlap as Nuvance's Norwalk Hospital). 700+ physicians; teaching affiliation with Donald and Barbara Zucker School of Medicine at Hofstra/Northwell. [Source 14]
  • Northwell-system position: NWH joined Northwell Health in January 2015 — coincident with the Schwartzes' $1M surgical gift, which means the Schwartz relationship straddles the pre-Northwell (independent) and post-Northwell (system) eras at this hospital. Northwell-owned ("is owned by Northwell Health" per the hospital's own description) rather than affiliate-only. [Source 14]
  • Why this matters for Nuvance: Northern Westchester is the closest structural analog to Norwalk Hospital inside the Northwell footprint — a wealthy-suburban community hospital that joined a larger system (~decade after Norwalk's analogous transition) and retained donor identity through the integration. Norwalk's catchment (Fairfield County) is wealthier than NWH's (Northern Westchester/Putnam), but the hospital scale (245 beds NWH vs. ~366 beds Norwalk) and service-line ambition (trauma, neuro, behavioral health) are directly comparable.
  • Case for support at time of gift: NWH was building toward NY State Level III-N trauma designation (specialized treatment including traumatic brain injury), which the region lacked — patients were being transported out for severe trauma, stroke, and complex seizure care. The hospital had already received the gala-record $2.5M (Nov 2024) for pediatric behavioral health on a $7M campaign; the trauma case was the next discrete program-level case the Foundation could put in front of principal donors. [Sources 5, 13]
  • Existing donor base: Wealthy Northern Westchester families (Bedford, Chappaqua, Mount Kisco, Pound Ridge, Katonah). Notable peer-donor presence includes Jane Donaldson (co-chaired 2024 gala with Lisa Schwartz), Angela Kessel (foundation board chair, "Our Mission Your Health" capital campaign co-chair), Gary Cohen (Community Board chair, Northwell Health Board). [Sources 5, 13]
  • Foundation/Development infrastructure: NWH Foundation operates under Northwell Foundation umbrella but maintains a local president — Keeva Young-Wright (Associate Executive Director of NWH and President of NWH Foundation). System-level CDO is Amelia J. Alverson (EVP/Chief Development Officer, Northwell Foundation, who joined in October 2025 from 16 years at Columbia, succeeding Brian T. Lally after 12 years leading the Northwell Foundation). This hybrid local-Foundation-president + system-CDO structure is the Northwell template that Nuvance is now inheriting post-merger. [Source 15]

4. The cultivation story

The cultivation arc breaks into four phases, the last of which is the only one that "feels" like cultivation in the textbook sense — and even that compressed phase is unintelligible without the prior 24 years of governance and program engagement.

Phase 1 — Pre-Northwell board pipeline (early 2000s through 2014). Mark Schwartz joined the NWH Board of Trustees in the early 2000s while still active at Goldman Sachs and Soros; he served three terms and rose to Vice-Chair. Lisa, in parallel but informally, helped establish the hospital's first nursing professional development endowment in 2000. This board-couple structure — husband as governance director, wife as program champion — is the same divided-labor model the Schwartzes deploy across their other affiliations (NYP, MGH, Harvard, MIT). [Sources 8, 9, 11]

Phase 2 — Surgical bridge gift, system transition (2015). In February 2015, the same month NWH joined Northwell Health, the Schwartzes announced a $1M gift to enable the hospital's surgical modernization (six new operating rooms opened in 2016 with ceiling-mounted technology). The press materials explicitly cite Mark's "three-term member and Vice-Chair" role on the NWH Board; the gift was, in effect, a vote of confidence in the Northwell integration. This gift was the first time the public name on a Schwartz NWH gift exceeded six figures. [Source 8]

Phase 3 — Lisa Schwartz formal trustee appointment + Community Foundation co-chair (2018-2024). In 2018, Lisa was formally named to the NWH Board of Trustees alongside Daren Wu, MD (Open Door CMO) by then-Board Chair Michael Schwartz. She also joined the NWH Community Foundation Board and became co-chair of its Community Health Committee. This shifted the governance structure: Mark had cycled off; Lisa was now the inside-the-room trustee. Lisa's committee-level work surfaced the pediatric behavioral health gap as a Northern Westchester-specific case for support. The Schwartzes anchored a new $7M pediatric mental health campaign with a $1M lead gift, and Lisa co-chaired (with Jane Donaldson) the November 2, 2024 gala at the former Reader's Digest building in Chappaqua. That gala drew 700+ supporters and raised a record $2.5M, including a $631,000 paddle raise. The campaign had pledged $5.9M of $7M by gala night. [Sources 4, 9, 12, 13]

Phase 4 — The "What's next?" conversation (late November 2024 to April 2025). The crucial, publicly documented moment: "When Lisa and Mark Schwartz met with leaders at Northern Westchester Hospital in late November 2024, they had one question: 'What's next?'" [Source 2] Derek Anderson — NWH President since 2019, MPH/FACHE, Northwell veteran since 2010, chair of the Northern Metropolitan Hospital Association — initially thought the meeting was a gala debrief. [Sources 2, 16] The hospital was prepared. The case in the room: Level III-N trauma designation (NY State criteria), neurology/epilepsy expansion, neurovascular/stroke. Northwell senior clinical leaders were drawn in to validate the program-level depth — Souhel Najjar, MD (SVP of Neurology Services, Northwell) and Daniel Sciubba, MD, MBA (Chair/SVP of Neurosurgery Service Line, Northwell). System CEO Michael Dowling validated at the top. From "What's next?" to public announcement: approximately five months. [Sources 1, 2, 3]

The team in the room at the ask: Derek Anderson (NWH President — local relationship owner), Keeva Young-Wright (NWH Foundation President — operational owner), Michael Dowling (Northwell President/CEO — system validator), Najjar and Sciubba (clinical-leadership validators on the specific service lines being funded). This is the CEO + local president + Foundation president + clinical-leadership-pair composition that recurs across Northwell mega-gift announcements. [Sources 1, 2, 3]

Critical observation: The "ask" was not really an ask. Lisa Schwartz asked the hospital "What's next?" — meaning the institution had successfully positioned itself such that the trustee-donor was driving the next-gift conversation. This is the highest form of cultivation: when the donor pulls the program out of the institution.

5. The gift structure

  • One-time vs. multi-year: Announced as a $10M commitment to the trauma/neurology initiative. Public materials describe it as a current commitment to "fast-track" the work — implies cash/near-cash deployment rather than long-pledge structure, but exact payment schedule is not publicly disclosed. [Sources 1, 2, 3]
  • Cash vs. appreciated stock vs. DAF: Not publicly disclosed. Given Mark's Goldman/Soros wealth-source profile and the couple's pattern of multiple program-restricted gifts (suggesting flexible private-foundation or DAF infrastructure), some portion almost certainly routes through an appreciated-asset or DAF mechanism — but no public confirmation.
  • Naming rights: None publicly announced. The gift was framed entirely around program designations (Level III-N trauma; neurology/epilepsy; neurovascular/stroke) and operational outcomes (specialized staffing; infrastructure upgrades; recruiting a director of trauma). There is no "Schwartz Trauma Center" or "Schwartz Pavilion." [Sources 1, 2, 3, 5]
  • Restrictions/designations: Heavily restricted by program but flexible on use within those programs:
    1. Achieving Level III-N trauma designation (NY State Department of Health criteria — includes specialized TBI care, specialized staffing, infrastructure, advanced diagnostic tools including biplane angiography for stroke)
    2. Expanding neurology and epilepsy services
    3. Enhancing neurovascular and stroke care
    4. Infrastructure upgrades, specialized staffing, program development [Sources 1, 2, 3, 5]
  • Performance/contingency clauses: None publicly disclosed. The Level III-N designation is itself an external (state regulatory) performance milestone — the gift implicitly conditions success on achieving it.
  • Companion-campaign integration: The Foundation explicitly used the December 2025 follow-on gala ($2.2M raised, "Because every second matters" theme) to extend the trauma/neuroscience case among other donors, layered on top of the Schwartz lead gift. The Schwartzes' $10M was thus deployed not as a closing gift but as a launching gift to anchor a broader case. [Source 5]

6. Post-gift stewardship and follow-on

  • Post-gift stewardship moves:
    • April 24, 2025 announcement coordinated across Northwell central comms, NWH local comms, regional press (River Journal, Westfair, Lifestyles Magazine), and the Northwell Foundation "Pacesetters" donor-spotlight program. Schwartzes received a permanent profile page on the Northwell Foundation site framing them as exemplars. [Sources 1, 2, 3, 7, 9, 17]
    • December 2025 follow-on gala ("Because every second matters") explicitly built on the Schwartz trauma case and raised an additional $2.2M from 600 attendees, with honors going to Angela Kessel and Gary Cohen (peer-donor recognition that keeps the broader donor cohort engaged around the same case). [Source 5]
    • Northwell senior leadership (Dowling, Najjar, Sciubba, Anderson) all on the record by name on the trauma case, providing the Schwartzes with the institutional voice-of-authority backing they signal as valuing. [Sources 1, 2, 3]
  • Follow-on giving: This $10M itself functions as a follow-on to the 2024 pediatric behavioral health $1M anchor + nursing endowment + 2015 surgical gift. The Schwartzes' 25-year pattern is roughly one anchor gift per emerging-need cycle — the next follow-on would be expected to come when NWH identifies the next emerging clinical gap, plausibly in the post-trauma-Level-III-N period (2027-2028).
  • Controversies/course-corrections: None publicly documented. The gift has been received with uniformly positive press coverage.

7. What worked — patterns to extract

  1. Multi-decade trustee pipeline beats discrete cultivation. The cultivation that landed the $10M gift began in 2000, not 2024. Mark Schwartz's three-term board service and Vice-Chair tenure built the institutional knowledge and trust. Lisa's 2018 trustee appointment kept the inside-the-room seat warm after Mark cycled off. The November 2024 meeting was the harvest of a 25-year planting. Transferability: Nuvance must staff its multi-decade trustee pipelines today for harvests in the 2030s and beyond — but it must also identify which existing donors have already done the 10+ years of board work and are ready for the harvest meeting now.

  2. Husband-as-governance / wife-as-program division of labor. Mark sat on the board and made the institution credible to him; Lisa drove the program-specific work (nursing endowment 2000; behavioral health committee 2018+). When she asked "What's next?", she was asking on the strength of her own program-side credibility, not as a spouse. Transferability: Recognize couple-donors' actual division of labor and engage each individually on the side they care about. Do not default to engaging only the "Bloomberg-listed" spouse.

  3. The trustee asks the institution what's next, not the other way around. This is the single most replicable insight. By the time of the November 2024 meeting, the hospital had positioned itself so that the trustee-donor was pulling the next case for support out of the institution. This requires (a) clinical leadership credible enough to articulate the next gap (Najjar/Sciubba/Anderson did this), (b) a Foundation infrastructure ready with a vetted case (the trauma case was pre-baked), and (c) a trustee with enough institutional knowledge to ask the question. Transferability: Nuvance should engineer "What's next?" moments with Bauer (post-Pavilion), Whittingham (post-cancer), Goldman/Fowler (post-initial-gift) — but only after they have institutional knowledge sufficient to ask.

  4. Anchor the unfunded; escalate when it's working. Lisa explicitly identifies this as the Schwartz philosophy: "Mark and I tend to be drawn to projects and ideas that are emerging and that need funding, but no one is there yet." The 2024 pediatric behavioral health $1M anchor preceded the 2025 $10M trauma anchor — both targeted emerging unmet clinical needs. Transferability: Position Nuvance's growth-stage clinical programs (behavioral health, advanced cancer, neuro/stroke at Norwalk) as the unfunded-emerging-need cases that match this donor pattern.

  5. Program designation, not naming rights. Zero public naming associated with the $10M. This is a critical signal about what this donor cohort actually values. Transferability: Bauer's $20M Norwalk gift was naming-anchored (Bauer Family Pavilion); Whittingham's giving has historically been naming-anchored. But the Schwartz pattern suggests Nuvance should not assume escalation gifts at the $10M+ tier require naming. Some donor profiles — particularly finance-derived wealth with multi-institution trustee portfolios — explicitly prefer program over name.

  6. Compressed 5-month close cycle after 24-year preparation. The "What's next?" conversation was November 2024; the announcement was April 24, 2025. Five months. This is not a counterexample to long cultivation — it is the payoff of long cultivation. Transferability: Once a Nuvance donor signals "what's next?" energy, the institution must be ready to close in 90-180 days. Slow institutional response after the donor opens the door is the most replicable failure mode.

  7. Clinical-leadership credibility at the ask, not just CEO-level. Najjar (neurology) and Sciubba (neurosurgery) appear in the foundational announcement materials by name. Their presence converts a fundraising conversation into a clinical-strategy conversation. Transferability: Nuvance should never send a single executive to a $10M+ conversation. The clinical service-line chair (e.g., Whittingham Cancer Center director, Norwalk neuro chief) must be in the room.

8. What didn't work or could have

  • What's missing from public record: Despite the depth of public materials, no source documents what failed in the Schwartz cultivation between 2015 and 2024. The gap between the $1M (2015) and the $1M behavioral health anchor (2024) is nine years. Was there an unsuccessful intermediate ask? An institutional transition (Joel Seligman's retirement and Derek Anderson's 2019 ascent) that paused the relationship? No public source explains the gap.
  • Implied institutional lesson — leadership-transition risk: NWH's executive director transition from Joel Seligman to Derek Anderson in 2019 occurred during the Schwartzes' "quiet years" — not coincidentally. Northwell's institutional response was to elevate Anderson (a Northwell-system veteran since 2010 with progression through North Shore University Hospital) rather than recruit externally, preserving cultural continuity. The Schwartzes' major-gift activity resumed after Anderson had been in role for 3-4 years. Transferability: Nuvance must minimize donor-relationship discontinuity during executive transitions — particularly the current period of post-merger integration where Norwalk Hospital leadership and NWH-style Foundation infrastructure are still being wired in.
  • Naming-rights ambiguity as future risk: The absence of naming on the $10M is a strength for current donor satisfaction but a risk for future fundraising — it leaves the marquee Level III-N trauma center un-named and potentially available to a future principal donor. The Schwartzes appear to have explicitly chosen this, but it does limit the gift's stewardship-fuel for the next 10-15 years (no annual "Schwartz Trauma Center" press cycle).

9. Applicability to Nuvance

This case is the most direct Northwell-system analog at the scale Nuvance is targeting. Specific transfer paths:

A. Bauer escalation at Norwalk Hospital (top-priority transfer).

  • Bauer's $20M was naming-anchored (Bauer Family Pavilion, opens winter 2025-2026).
  • The Schwartz pattern suggests the post-Pavilion escalation gift should be program-anchored, not building-anchored, and should target the next emerging clinical case for support at Norwalk (neuro/stroke, advanced cancer beyond Whittingham, behavioral health expansion).
  • The "What's next?" question should be engineered for Bauer in late 2026 or early 2027, once the Pavilion is open and operational and the Bauers are in stewardship momentum.
  • Composition of the ask: Norwalk Hospital president + Nuvance/Northwell CDO + relevant service-line chief + Northwell system validator (Dowling-tier).
  • Bauer's analog risk: Unlike the Schwartzes, Bauer has not (yet) put a family member on the Nuvance board. This is a gap. Nuvance should evaluate whether a Bauer-family next-generation trustee appointment is feasible to replicate the Schwartz governance-pipeline model.

B. Whittingham escalation at Norwalk/Whittingham Cancer Center.

  • Whittingham giving has historically been naming-anchored. The Schwartz pattern suggests the next escalation gift could be program-restricted to a sub-specialty expansion (e.g., proton therapy, precision oncology, cancer genomics) within the existing Whittingham name umbrella — separating the name commitment from the escalation commitment.
  • Compose the cultivation team to include Northwell cancer service-line leadership (post-merger) — replicate the Najjar/Sciubba clinical-validator structure that landed the Schwartz gift.

C. Goldman/Fowler relationship-building.

  • Goldman/Fowler is in early/mid-cultivation. The Schwartz case shows that the high-leverage moves at this stage are not gift solicitations but board/committee placement — getting a Goldman/Fowler family member onto a Nuvance committee where they will, over 3-5 years, build the institutional knowledge to eventually ask "What's next?"
  • Lisa Schwartz's 2018 trustee appointment was, in retrospect, the single highest-leverage cultivation move Northwell made for this donor in the post-2015 period.

D. Northwell-system donor relationships post-merger.

  • Phase 0 verification noted Northwell's $1B/5-year investment commitment across Nuvance facilities. The Schwartz case shows that Northwell's system-level fundraising infrastructure (Northwell Foundation under Amelia Alverson) is now the institutional home for Nuvance's principal-gift work — but the relationship ownership stays local (Derek Anderson at NWH; Norwalk Hospital president at Norwalk).
  • Nuvance should expect its top donors to receive the "Pacesetters"-style stewardship treatment that the Schwartzes received post-gift — and should pre-position its leading prospects for that treatment.

E. Trustee-pipeline model (system-wide transferability).

  • The single most actionable transfer: build a 10-25-year trustee/committee pipeline at every Nuvance hospital that mirrors the Schwartz arc (governance role → committee chair → program anchor → escalation gift). Identify candidates today who are at year 2-5 of this arc and accelerate them.

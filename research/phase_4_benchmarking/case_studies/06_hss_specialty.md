# Case Study — Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) specialty-institute model: cultivating transformational donors at a single-specialty hospital

**Gift size (representative anchor):** $35M Kellen Tower (2021); ~$60M cumulative Kellen Foundation over the decade prior [1][2]
**Type:** Principal capital + repeat major-gift program at a single-specialty hospital
**Compiled:** 2026-05-11

## 1. The gift in one paragraph

In March 2021, the Anna-Maria and Stephen Kellen Foundation announced a $35M lead gift to name the 12-story, ~100,000-square-foot **Anna-Maria and Stephen Kellen Medical Tower** at the Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) — a tower built over FDR Drive at 71st Street to house complex joint replacement and spine care. The announcement disclosed that the Foundation's cumulative HSS support totaled "almost $60 million over the last decade," and that Marina Kellen French (Foundation VP and HSS Trustee) had built the relationship through a 30-year friendship with surgeon Thomas Sculco, MD, and over a decade of board service. [1][2][7] The Kellen Tower opened in March 2026, completing HSS's largest capital expansion to date. [3][8] This case study uses the Kellen relationship — alongside parallel relationships with Kenneth C. Griffin ($12M, 2024), the Stavros Niarchos Foundation ($10M, 2023; $10M, 2018; 20-year partnership), the Alfred and Norma Lerner family (Lerner Children's Pavilion, 2012), and Greenwich-corridor donors Andreas and Diane Halvorsen ($5M, 2021) and Patty & Jay Baker ($20M match, 2023) — to extract the specialty-institute playbook that lets a 215-bed orthopedic-only hospital cultivate principal gifts at scale. [3][4][5][6][9][13]

## 2. Donor profile

The HSS principal-donor cohort is not one donor — it is a *type*. The case study spans five primary archetypes that recur in HSS's named-gift register and that together explain the institution's transformational-gift capacity.

**Type A — The grateful patient who becomes a board member who becomes a transformational donor.** Patty & Jay Baker are the textbook case: Jay Baker (Naples-based philanthropist and former Kohl's executive) had successful surgery at HSS in New York; his wife Patty subsequently joined the HSS Board of Trustees; the couple then made a **$20M match commitment** to anchor the new HSS Florida orthopedic center at Naples Comprehensive Health (NCH), and the Patty & Jay Baker Pavilion broke ground in November 2023. [13][14] The cultivation pathway — grateful-patient experience at the New York flagship → board service → transformational gift back to an HSS-branded facility in the donor's home market — is the model HSS has industrialized. Halvorsen sits in the same archetype: Diane and Andreas Halvorsen (Darien, CT; Viking Global) made a $5M gift in 2021 to create the **Halvorsen Wellness Center**, a facility for HSS staff emotional and physical health, after years of being described in HSS materials as "dedicated champions of physician and staff wellness." [6]

**Type B — The European-rooted family foundation operating as a multi-generational benefactor.** The Anna-Maria and Stephen Kellen Foundation is the canonical case: founded on the wealth of Stephen Kellen (a German émigré who became chairman of Arnhold & S. Bleichroeder) and his wife Anna-Maria, with their daughter Marina Kellen French managing the philanthropic agenda. Marina Kellen French has served on the HSS Board of Trustees and Board of Advisors for "more than 10 years" and personally created the **Anna-Maria and Stephen Kellen Clinician-Scientist Program** and endowed the **Marina Kellen Chair in Complex Joint Reconstruction** — naming opportunities at three different scales (program, chair, tower). [1][7] Prince's-framework classification: **Dynast** (multi-generational foundation), with strong **Investor** overtones (the gift was explicitly framed by board co-chairs as "an investment in healthcare when it is needed most"). [1]

**Type C — The single-cause-curious mega-donor (Repayer/Communitarian).** Kenneth C. Griffin's $12M multi-year gift in June 2024 to establish the **Kenneth C. Griffin Research Accelerator** at HSS is the case. Griffin had "previously supported vital investigations in knee replacement surgery and precision medicine, as well as the expansion of facilities for care and research at HSS" — i.e., his $12M gift was the culmination of staged, smaller prior gifts. [4][9] Prince's classification: a **Repayer** with knee-condition affinity, plus heavy **Communitarian** (Griffin's broader $545M+ multi-system healthcare-philanthropy program). [9]

**Type D — The international foundation building a global musculoskeletal-care platform.** The Stavros Niarchos Foundation has supported HSS for more than 20 years, including a 2018 $10M gift naming the **Stavros Niarchos Foundation Complex Joint Reconstruction Center**, training programs that have brought 160+ Greek orthopedic surgeons to HSS, and a 2023 $10M Kellen Tower gift naming the SNF Digital Inpatient Experience floors. [5] Prince's classification: **Investor** (return on philanthropic capital measured in global capacity-building) + **Dynast** (Stavros Niarchos Foundation as multi-generational mission). [5]

**Type E — The historical principal benefactor establishing the brand.** Alfred Lerner (MBNA founder, Cleveland Browns owner, b. 1933 d. 2002; Forbes #36 in 2002 at $4.3B) and his wife Norma's family enabled the **Alfred and Norma Lerner Children's Pavilion** that opened October 2012 as a "hospital within a hospital" for pediatric orthopedics. [10] The Lerner family is documented elsewhere as having made a separate **$100M gift to the Cleveland Clinic** (one of the largest single gifts in academic-medicine history at the time), giving the Lerner-HSS pavilion archetype the strongest possible peer-cohort signal. [11]

The pattern across all five types: HSS does not have an academic medical center's research scale, federal-grant footprint, or medical-school halo to attract donors. Instead, it has cultivated five distinct *motivational gateways* into a single shared identity — the world's #1 orthopedic specialty hospital — and built capital opportunities sized to each gateway.

## 3. Recipient institution profile (at time of representative gifts, 2018-2024)

HSS is a 215-bed, 58-operating-room, ~450-staff specialty hospital founded in 1863 as "the Hospital of the New York Society for the Relief of the Ruptured and Crippled" and renamed in 1940 to Hospital for Special Surgery. [3] Following its 1949 affiliation with Cornell University Medical College, HSS eliminated all non-orthopedic and non-rheumatologic surgical departments — a deliberate, century-long *narrowing* into a single-specialty identity. [3]

**Scale and clinical positioning at the time of the Kellen gift (2021):**
- ~30,000 surgical procedures annually; HSS performs more hip surgeries and more knee replacements than any other US hospital [15]
- Ranked **#1 in orthopedics nationally** by U.S. News & World Report for 15 consecutive years [3]
- Ranked **#1 worldwide in orthopedics** by Newsweek 2021-2025 [3]
- Ranked **#3 in rheumatology** by U.S. News & World Report 2024-2025 [3]
- 2024 annual revenue: ~$719M; total assets ~$3.4B [12]
- Research enterprise with 217 patents held as of 2024; NIH-designated specialized centers for arthritis/musculoskeletal disease (1988) and lupus (1993) [3]

**Case for support (as articulated by HSS in 2018-2024 campaign and donor materials):**

The HSS case-for-support is built on five claims that work *because* HSS is single-specialty, not despite it:

1. **The mission is universal.** "Musculoskeletal conditions affect more than one out of every two adults in the US and nearly 75% of those age 65 and over." Movement is positioned as a universal human good ("Movement is a gift"). [16]
2. **The volume-outcomes connection is empirically demonstrable.** HSS's surgical volume (79 procedures/day average, 365/year average per surgeon) translates into the lowest infection rate in New York State and significantly lower readmissions than national peers — a measurable competitive advantage that scales every additional dollar of capital. [15]
3. **Care standardization works because the scope is narrow.** HSS deploys "Comprehensive Care Pathways" (clinical protocols) and a clinical registry of 84,000+ patients — infrastructure that academic medical centers struggle to build across heterogeneous service lines. [15]
4. **The specialty model can replicate.** HSS Florida (West Palm Beach + Wellington + Jupiter satellites + the Naples NCH partnership) and the HSS-NCH Patty & Jay Baker Pavilion (a 100,000-square-foot orthopedic hospital opening 2025) demonstrate that HSS-brand specialty care can be exported. [13][14]
5. **Research dollars compound through specialty depth.** HSS frames philanthropy around five priorities: General Support, Research, Rheumatology, Capital Expansion, Virtual Health. Each is bounded to musculoskeletal disease. [16]

HSS conducted a "Your Life: Our Mission" comprehensive campaign in the mid-2010s with a $300M goal that ultimately raised approximately $320M, per institutional accounts. [17] Following completion, HSS pivoted to project-specific transformational asks (Kellen Tower, Griffin Research Accelerator, Halvorsen Wellness Center, Baker Pavilion) rather than another comprehensive multi-year campaign — an approach better matched to a focused single-specialty identity. [1][4][6][13]

**Foundation/development infrastructure:** Two-tier governance with a ~59-member Board of Trustees co-chaired by Thomas H. Lister (Permira) and Robert K. Steel (former NYC Deputy Mayor for Economic Development; former CEO of Wachovia), plus 15 Life Trustees, a 40+ member Board of Advisors co-chaired by Katherine Doerge and Robert D. Yaffa, and an International Advisory Council. [18] Recognizable trustees include Jonathan M. Tisch (hospitality), Steven Rattner (investment), Jeff Wilpon (Mets ownership), Marina Kellen French (Kellen Foundation), Patty Baker (HSS Florida lead donor); Board of Advisors includes Venus Williams and Lara Spencer (representing the sports-and-celebrity halo HSS cultivates as part of its public identity). [18] The annual HSS Tribute Dinner (39 editions through 2024) functions as both a fundraising vehicle (raising $5-6M/year in event revenue) and the formal recognition platform for transformational donors — Griffin's $12M gift was announced at the 2024 Tribute Dinner. [4][19]

## 4. The cultivation story

The HSS cultivation playbook can be reconstructed from the public record of multiple named gifts and shows a consistent four-stage arc.

**Stage 1 — Clinical encounter (months to years).** A high-net-worth individual or a member of their family receives care at HSS for a musculoskeletal condition. Because HSS is the #1 destination for joint replacement, complex spine, and elite-athlete orthopedics, the clinical pipeline includes a structurally high proportion of wealthy patients. Jay Baker's successful surgery at the New York flagship is the documented entry point for what became a $20M Naples gift. [13]

**Stage 2 — Physician-mediated relationship-building (years).** The cultivation does not run through development staff in isolation; it runs through the treating physician. Marina Kellen French's $35M tower gift, the Kellen Foundation's ~$60M cumulative giving, the Anna-Maria and Stephen Kellen Clinician-Scientist Program, and the Marina Kellen Chair were all described in HSS materials as flowing from "a 30-year friendship between Marina Kellen French and Dr. Thomas Sculco" (Surgeon-in-Chief Emeritus, Director of the SNF Complex Joint Reconstruction Center). [1] This is the orthopedic-specialty equivalent of grateful-patient cultivation in oncology: the surgeon-donor dyad is the fundamental unit, and HSS structures itself to preserve and lengthen that relationship.

**Stage 3 — Board service (typically several years before the major gift).** Marina Kellen French served on HSS's Board of Trustees and Board of Advisors for "more than 10 years" prior to the $35M tower gift. [1] Patty Baker joined the HSS Board of Trustees before the $20M Naples gift. [13] HSS uses board membership as a cultivation runway — not as the closing move but as the trust-building intermediate stage where the donor is socialized into the institution's strategic priorities, peer-cohort of fellow trustees, and decision-making rhythms.

**Stage 4 — The ask, anchored to a named capital opportunity and announced at the Tribute Dinner or institutional press event.** Lou Shapiro (CEO 2007-2023, replaced by surgeon-in-chief Bryan T. Kelly, MD, in 2023) personally led the Kellen Tower ask alongside board co-chairs Lister and Steel; Griffin's $12M gift was unveiled at the 2024 Tribute Dinner where he was honored. [1][4][20] The structure of the announcement — physician honoree paired with philanthropist honoree, with the gift named for the donor and tied to a specific named asset (tower, accelerator, pavilion, wellness center) — is consistent across the Kellen, Griffin, Niarchos, Lerner, Halvorsen, and Baker announcements.

**Timeline (approximate, where reconstructable from public sources):**
- Kellen-HSS relationship: ~30 years (friendship with Sculco) → 10+ years (board service) → $35M tower naming (2021), with $60M cumulative across the decade prior [1][7]
- SNF-HSS relationship: ~20 years (since 2006 Greek surgeon seminar) → 2018 $10M CJRC naming gift → 2023 $10M Kellen Tower digital-experience naming [5]
- Griffin-HSS relationship: prior smaller gifts to knee surgery and precision medicine → June 2024 $12M Research Accelerator [4]
- Baker-HSS relationship: clinical encounter at NYC flagship → Patty Baker HSS Board trustee role → November 2023 $20M match gift for Naples Baker Pavilion [13]

## 5. The gift structure

HSS uses five gift-structure conventions consistently across the documented principal gifts.

**(a) Multi-year pledges, not one-time cash transfers.** The Kellen $35M was for a tower whose construction spanned 2021-2026 (opened March 2026); Griffin's $12M is explicitly multi-year; the SNF gifts are layered over a 20-year relationship. [1][4][5] The pledge structure aligns donor recognition with construction timelines and gives the donor multiple visible milestones (groundbreaking, topping-out, opening, dedication) at which to deepen public association with the project.

**(b) Family-foundation gift vehicles over personal-checkbook gifts.** Kellen Foundation (not Marina Kellen French personally), Stavros Niarchos Foundation, Halvorsen Family Foundation, Kapnick Foundation, and the Lerner Foundation are the named-gift vehicles in the HSS record. [1][5][6][13] This reflects the dynast-archetype donor population HSS attracts and gives the donor family a recurring institutional voice that survives generational transition.

**(c) Layered naming opportunities at multiple scales.** Within a single family relationship, HSS offers naming at three or four scales: a clinician-scientist *program*, an endowed *chair*, a clinical *center* or *floor* within a tower, and the *tower itself*. The Kellen Foundation's HSS portfolio illustrates: Anna-Maria and Stephen Kellen Clinician-Scientist Program (program-level), Marina Kellen Chair in Complex Joint Reconstruction (chair-level), Anna-Maria and Stephen Kellen Medical Tower (tower-level), plus the interdisciplinary collaboration with Mayo Clinic on arthrofibrosis (initiative-level). [7] This sequencing lets the donor's giving deepen over time without negotiating a single new naming each time.

**(d) Named gifts tied to a defined clinical or research deliverable, not unrestricted operating support.** Each principal gift in the HSS register named a specific clinical or research mission: knee research (Griffin Research Accelerator); complex joint reconstruction (SNF CJRC, Marina Kellen Chair); staff wellness (Halvorsen Wellness Center); pediatrics (Lerner Children's Pavilion); orthopedic care delivery in Florida (Baker Pavilion). [1][4][5][6][10][13] HSS does not lead with unrestricted asks at the principal-gift level.

**(e) Matching-gift structures used for scaling capital projects.** The Baker $20M was structured as a *match* on additional fundraising at Naples Comprehensive Health, multiplying the $20M into a higher final total. [13] The HSS Florida-NCH partnership combines the $20M Baker match with $7.5M from the Kapnick Foundation and additional contributions to fund the 100,000-square-foot Patty & Jay Baker Pavilion. [14]

**Tax/financial structuring:** Public sources do not disclose the cash/stock/DAF mix for individual HSS gifts. HSS's planned-giving page actively cultivates DAF gifts and charitable lead trusts, consistent with the high-net-worth-individual donor population the institution targets.

## 6. Post-gift stewardship and follow-on

The strongest evidence for HSS's stewardship effectiveness is the *repeat-gift density* in its principal-donor portfolio. The Kellen Foundation made ~$60M cumulative across the decade *before* the 2021 $35M tower naming, and Marina Kellen French personally gave an additional $3.5M in 2020 (one year before the tower announcement) to fund TKA research — a sequential layering that suggests stewardship pathways were operating continuously. [1][7]

The SNF-HSS partnership demonstrates the same pattern at the institutional-foundation level: a 2018 $10M gift naming the SNF Complex Joint Reconstruction Center, followed by ongoing operational support (the SNF Greek-surgeon visiting fellowship), then another $10M gift in 2023 for Kellen Tower digital-experience floors — three discrete principal-tier gifts within a single 20-year cultivation arc. [5]

Griffin's $12M 2024 gift was explicitly framed as building on prior support: "Mr. Griffin has previously supported vital investigations in knee replacement surgery and precision medicine, as well as the expansion of facilities for care and research at HSS." [4] HSS announced the gift at the Tribute Dinner where Griffin was the honoree — a recognition platform that pairs the recognition moment with peer-cohort visibility.

**No public controversies surface in the post-gift period for any of the documented major gifts.** Unlike academic medical centers that have faced donor-name controversies (Sackler removals at multiple institutions), HSS's donor naming has remained stable and uncontested across the 15-year period studied.

## 7. What worked — patterns to extract

**Pattern A — Narrow scope *enables* (rather than constrains) transformational giving.** The conventional intuition is that an academic medical center's breadth attracts more donors because it offers more entry points. HSS demonstrates the inverse: because every donor knows exactly what HSS is (the #1 musculoskeletal hospital in the world), the value proposition is *unambiguous* at the ask. Donors do not have to choose among competing missions or worry their gift will be cross-subsidized to unrelated programs. The narrow scope is itself a fundraising asset.

**Pattern B — The surgeon-donor dyad is the cultivation primitive.** Across the Kellen, Griffin, and SNF relationships, the donor relationship is anchored to a named treating physician (Dr. Thomas Sculco in the Kellen case; the surgical leadership team in the Griffin case). HSS preserves long surgeon tenures (Sculco was Surgeon-in-Chief and is now Surgeon-in-Chief Emeritus) and uses the physician-donor relationship as the institutional asset that survives leadership transitions at the CEO/board level.

**Pattern C — Three- or four-scale naming menus inside a single donor relationship.** Rather than offering one large naming opportunity per donor, HSS structures the relationship to offer program, chair, center, and tower-scale naming options sequentially, letting the donor's commitment deepen without negotiating a new naming concept each cycle. This is structurally similar to the multi-generational naming pattern at academic medical centers but executed within a single specialty's depth rather than across multiple specialties.

**Pattern D — The Tribute Dinner as recognition-and-cultivation engine.** The annual Tribute Dinner is simultaneously a fundraising event ($5-6M/year), a peer-cohort assembly (trustees, surgeons, and prior major donors gathered annually), and the announcement venue for transformational gifts that have just closed. The pairing of physician honorees with philanthropist honorees signals to the room that medicine and philanthropy are co-equal contributions — an institutional posture that flatters major donors and cements their identification with the hospital's mission.

**Pattern E — Specialty replication exports the brand to where the donors live.** HSS Florida (West Palm Beach + Jupiter + Wellington + Naples NCH) is not merely a clinical expansion — it is a philanthropic strategy. By establishing HSS-branded specialty facilities in Naples (where Jay & Patty Baker live), HSS converted a New York grateful-patient relationship into a $20M Naples gift and a new locally branded pavilion. The lesson: a specialty institute can replicate to where its grateful-patient donors retire or summer, multiplying the cultivation surface area without diluting the specialty identity.

**Pattern F — Board service as a cultivation runway, not the closing move.** Marina Kellen French and Patty Baker both held multi-year board roles *before* their largest gifts. HSS uses board membership to socialize the donor into the institution's strategic decision-making, peer-cohort, and capital-project pipeline — and treats the principal gift as the *outcome* of that socialization, not the precondition.

## 8. What didn't work or could have

The publicly available record shows few documented failures, but three institutional vulnerabilities are visible:

**(a) Single-specialty concentration risk.** HSS's revenue (~$719M in 2024) is entirely dependent on orthopedic and rheumatologic care. A disruption in either field — surgical-volume migration to ambulatory surgery centers, payer pushback on inpatient joint replacement, a major adverse outcome event — would simultaneously threaten clinical revenue *and* the philanthropic case. [12] An academic medical center can pivot the case-for-support to a different specialty during a downturn; HSS cannot.

**(b) Pediatric pavilion philanthropy is structurally smaller than adult orthopedic philanthropy.** The Lerner Children's Pavilion opened in 2012 with the Lerner family's gift (specific amount not disclosed publicly), but the pediatric service line at HSS is smaller in volume and revenue than the adult joint-replacement lines, and the major-gift pipeline correspondingly draws fewer transformational asks. The single-specialty model concentrates philanthropic depth on the adult orthopedic lines where volume and surgeon prominence are highest.

**(c) Leadership transition risk at the CEO level (managed but not eliminated).** Lou Shapiro served as CEO from 2007-2023 and personally led the Kellen Tower ask; Bryan T. Kelly, MD, MBA succeeded him in September 2023 as the first surgeon-in-chief to also become CEO. [20] The transition appears to have been managed gracefully — Griffin's $12M was announced 9 months after Kelly's CEO appointment — but the major-donor relationship inventory built by Shapiro and his predecessor will require continued cultivation across the transition. HSS's reliance on long surgeon tenures (Sculco's continuity) hedged the CEO transition, but the hedge requires institutional discipline to sustain.

**(d) The HSS Florida expansion creates internal philanthropic competition.** The HSS Florida-NCH partnership creates a second pool of HSS-branded naming opportunities (the Baker Pavilion, the Kapnick Foundation Ambulatory Surgical Center) that could in theory compete with the New York flagship for the same donor's wallet. To date HSS has managed this constructively (the Bakers are simultaneously New York grateful patients and Florida donors), but the Florida expansion is recent and the long-run effects on flagship philanthropy are not yet measurable.

## 9. Applicability to Nuvance

HSS is the most strategically rich case study in Phase 4 *for Nuvance*, for three reasons.

**First, the Halvorsen Family Foundation — a documented Greenwich-corridor philanthropy already active in our target geography — gave $5M to HSS for the Halvorsen Wellness Center in 2021.** [6] The Halvorsens are documented in Phase 1 research as Darien CT residents, Viking Global founders, and active K-12 / Connecticut education philanthropists with no documented Nuvance giving. [Phase 1 long-list source]. The $5M HSS staff-wellness gift demonstrates that the Halvorsens *are* willing to fund hospital infrastructure when the case for support is sharp and the recognition is appropriately scaled. The Halvorsen-HSS gift is the affirmative evidence that Greenwich-corridor billionaires will give to specialty programs even outside their primary education giving — *if the institution offers a credibly differentiated specialty case for support*.

**Second, HSS represents the single largest *outflow* of Greenwich-corridor specialty-hospital philanthropy that is technically *recapturable* by a credibly positioned Nuvance specialty institute.** Andreas Halvorsen, Bauer (a Nuvance donor at the Wilton-Norwalk axis, $35M cumulative), and the Greenwich-Darien financial-services cohort have demonstrated they will fund specialty health-care destinations. HSS is the existence proof of the model. Norwalk Hospital sits 30 miles from the HSS flagship and 5 miles from the Halvorsens' Darien residence. The geographic logic for Nuvance is favorable; the institutional logic (Nuvance is currently a community health system without a specialty-institute brand) is the binding constraint.

**Third, the HSS model is the *most operationally transferable* benchmark in Phase 4 to Nuvance's specific position post-merger.** Nuvance is not going to become an academic medical center. It is not going to develop a medical school. It is not going to compete with Yale-New Haven or Northwell flagships on research-enterprise scale. But Nuvance *could* position one or two of its existing strong service lines as specialty institutes within the Northwell system — and several of the specific moves HSS uses are within reach.

**Specific transferable moves:**

1. **Identify one or two service lines with genuine clinical and volume leadership in the Hudson Valley / Western CT footprint that could carry a specialty-institute brand.** The most defensible candidates are: **Whittingham Cancer Center** (Norwalk; already a named cancer center; could be positioned as the "Whittingham Institute" within Nuvance/Northwell); **Vassar Brothers cardiac surgery** (Nuvance is the only regional cardiac surgery center for the Hudson Valley per the Phase 0 audit); and **Dyson Center for Cancer Care at Vassar Brothers** (the only of its kind in the Mid-Hudson Valley per the audit). Each of these has the volume + outcomes + named-asset starting point that HSS leveraged.

2. **Build the surgeon-donor dyad as the cultivation primitive.** HSS's 30-year Sculco-Kellen friendship is the template. Nuvance development should map its highest-acuity, longest-tenured physicians at each candidate institute (oncologists at Whittingham; cardiac surgeons at Vassar Brothers) and structure the major-gift cultivation through those physician relationships, not through institutional generalists.

3. **Adopt the layered-naming menu (program, chair, center, institute).** Rather than offering a single naming at the most expensive level, Nuvance should construct a three- or four-scale naming menu within each candidate institute — letting a Halvorsen-tier donor begin with a $5M endowed chair, deepen to a $15M named center, and ultimately consider a $25-50M institute-level naming. This is the multi-decade cultivation pathway that HSS's Kellen relationship validates.

4. **Build a Tribute-Dinner-equivalent annual recognition event with paired physician + philanthropist honorees.** This is the lowest-cost-highest-leverage HSS move that Nuvance can implement immediately. The event simultaneously raises 6-figure event revenue, creates a peer-cohort assembly, and serves as the announcement venue for the next year's principal gift. Northwell's "Outpacing the Impossible" peer-cohort dynamics demonstrate this works at scale; HSS demonstrates it works at single-institute scale.

5. **Use board service as a cultivation runway.** Marina Kellen French served 10+ years; Patty Baker served before her $20M gift. Nuvance Foundation should be structuring trustee terms with explicit major-gift cultivation outcomes — not as a precondition for board service, but as the implicit institutional expectation that the principal gift follows from the trust-building of the board years.

**Which of the 10 Nuvance candidates this most applies to:** the HSS playbook is *least* applicable to Bauer (Bauer's giving pattern is already settled with the Norwalk Pavilion; the institute-level naming is not what Bauer needs at age 95). It is *most* applicable to Halvorsen directly (an existing HSS donor at $5M who could be cultivated at the $25M+ level for a Nuvance specialty institute if Nuvance can construct a credible case for support); Whittingham (the Whittingham Cancer Center could be reframed as the "Whittingham Institute" with the next-generation Whittingham principals as natural sustaining donors); and Dyson (the Dyson Center for Cancer Care at Vassar Brothers is already a named asset that could be scaled to institute-level branding). The Dalio gift to Nuvance ($5.1M for Career Readiness Academy) is *not* in this archetype — Dalio's affinity runs through workforce/education, not specialty clinical care.

**Critical caveat: Nuvance currently lacks the institutional precondition for the HSS model.** The Phase 0 case-for-support audit found that Nuvance does not currently publish a clearly articulated multi-year case for support, has no comprehensive campaign goal, and does not present specific signature transformational opportunities to major-gift donors. [Phase 0 audit]. The HSS playbook requires that institutional definitional work to be complete *before* the cultivation pathway can run. Adopting the HSS model is therefore not a quick win — it is a 3-5 year institutional repositioning that has to be sequenced after Nuvance completes its post-merger case-for-support work with Northwell.
